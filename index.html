The dryer eats towels before the dryer dies

Towel packaging prints a thread count and a price, and the dryer is the machine that decides how long either one matters. Consider two sets on the same laundry schedule: eight washes and drys a month, 96 cycles a year. Set A runs $14 a bath towel and starts shedding in month seven, when the loops flatten and the edges curl. Set B runs $29 and gets replaced at year four, when the binding gives out. Nobody prices the machine hours, and the machine hours are the whole comparison.

A 55 minute cotton cycle costs about 43 cents at the national average of 16 cents per kilowatt hour, so the annual dry cost lands near $41 per set. Set A also runs hotter longer as the fibers mat, which stretches later cycles to 60 minutes. Over four years Set A needs four replacements at $56 each, $224 in towels, plus the dryer time, about $165. Set B takes one purchase, $58 for two towels, plus the same $165 of dryer time. The cheap set quietly burns $166 more over the same four years, and almost all of the gap is electricity, not terry cloth.

Lint tells you which set is losing. A towel sheds heaviest in its first ten washes, and the lint screen fills accordingly. After that, a screen that keeps coating every cycle means the loops are shearing off faster than the weave can hold them, which is why the towel stops absorbing water even though it still looks whole. Water has to sit on a flattened surface instead of wicking into loops, so a towel can be dry to the touch and still bad at its one job.

Heat is the variable you control. High heat cuts cycle time roughly in half and shears loops for the privilege; low heat doubles the runtime and keeps the pile standing. For a set you plan to keep five years, the low setting plus a clean lint screen is the closest thing to a warranty the dryer offers, and it costs nothing but 25 extra minutes a load.
